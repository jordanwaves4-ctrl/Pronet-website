why hygiene

A well without handwashing is half a well.

Ghana has spent thirty years drilling. It has spent far less time on what happens between the pump and the mouth — and that is where most water-borne disease is decided.

Water drawn from a safe source can be unsafe by the time it is drunk: stored in an open bucket, handled with unwashed hands, ladled with a cup that has been on the floor. Add a latrine sited uphill from the well, and a single heavy rain undoes the drilling.

The three things we treat as one problem

Water supply. A functioning point within a reasonable walk, with enough yield in the dry season. See boreholes and mechanisation.

Sanitation. A latrine the household actually uses, sited away from the water source and downslope of it. See sanitation & latrines.

Hygiene. Handwashing at the critical moments, safe storage, food handling and keeping waste away from the compound. See hygiene education.

How we teach it

Our social staff work in the language of the community, at times the community chooses — early morning before the market, or evening after the nets are mended. Sessions are demonstrations, not leaflets: a tippy-tap built from a jerrycan, a handwashing station placed where people already pass.

Every programme trains local volunteers to carry the message after we leave, and every school we build in gets a hygiene club run by teachers and pupils rather than by us.

How we know it worked

We return at three months and twelve months and look at the same four things: is there soap and water at the handwashing point, is drinking water covered, is the latrine in use, and has anyone in the household had diarrhoea in the last two weeks.

The numbers go into the same public record as our water-point functionality data. Where hygiene practice has slipped, the partner sees it in the report — and we go back.

Hygiene education is the cheapest line in our budget and the one most often left out of a drilling contract. It is the line we argue hardest for.
